How Walt picks a domino

Walt is this project's computer player for Texas 42. This page takes one of its decisions apart. It starts with the answer, goes down through each layer of the calculation to the random play at the bottom, then climbs back up.

The answer

Walt won the bid at 30, named fives as trump, and leads to the first trick. These are its seven dominoes. Under each one is Walt's estimate of the chance its team takes at least 30 of the hand's 42 points if it leads that domino. That chance is what Walt maximizes; the project calls it pmake.

Fives are trump. A heavier center bar marks a trump; the 5-0 is the only count domino in this hand, worth 5 points. These numbers are the real Walt's, from a saved decision record.

Walt led the 4-4: its team made 30 in 39 of 40 tries. The 5-0, a low trump that carries 5 points, scores worst at 19 of 40, about a coin flip. Where do numbers like "39 of 40" come from?

Forty imagined deals

Walt sees its own seven dominoes and nothing else. The other 21 are split among Left, Partner and Right in a way it can't see. So Walt makes up 40 deals: it shuffles those 21 dominoes and hands seven to each seat, 40 times over.

Then, for every possible lead and every imagined deal, Walt plays the whole hand out and checks one thing: did its team reach 30? Each square below is one of those checks. A row's count of makes is that domino's percentage.

From here on, the numbers come from a re-creation of Walt's method that runs in this page. It draws its own 40 deals, so its counts differ a little from the real Walt's. The ranking holds: 4-4 near the top, 5-0 at the bottom.

Inside a modeled player

A modeled player knows what a real player in that seat would know: its own dominoes and everything played so far. It then does what Walt does, on a smaller scale. It imagines 8 deals instead of 40, and scores each domino it could legally play on those 8.

The bottom layer is dice

Inside those 8 guesses, who plays for the other three seats? Nobody does any thinking. Each of them plays a random legal domino, as if rolling a die. Only the modeled player itself chooses with care, and it can't see through the dice either: its later plays have to work across all 8 guesses at once.

Random play still separates the options

Bidders' makes out of 8, one dot per fresh set of guesses

Sometimes the 8 guesses can't separate the options at all: every choice gets the same count, often 0 of 8 or 8 of 8. Then the modeled player falls back on a fixed rule and takes the lowest-numbered domino. Along the 4-4 line in these 40 deals, that happened in 117 of the 489 modeled decisions that had a real choice.

Where Walt asks the modeled players

Back up to Walt's own search. The diagram follows the 4-4 lead through the first two tricks in all 40 deals at once. Each column is one deal.

Whenever it's Left's, Partner's or Right's turn, Walt asks a modeled player for that seat what it would play. It asks once per deal, since each deal gives that seat different dominoes. Where the answers differ, the deals split apart.

Walt's own plays work the other way: one choice per group. Inside a group, every deal looks the same from Walt's chair, with the same dominoes played in the same order. Walt can't play one way in deal 3 and another in deal 17 unless something on the table tells those deals apart. That rule keeps the plan honest: no peeking at hidden hands.

Back to the top

Now each of the seven percentages can be read all the way down:

Walt imagines 40 deals and plays each lead to the end. Its later plays follow one plan that can't peek at the hidden hands. The percentage is the share of deals where its team reaches 30.
Each other seat is a modeled player. At every turn it imagines 8 deals of its own and plays whatever does best for its side across them.
Inside those 8 deals, everyone else plays a random legal domino. Random play still punishes giving away a count or a trick, so even this bottom layer pushes the modeled players toward sensible plays.
the real Waltthis page's re-creation
Re-running takes a few seconds. It runs the whole stack in your browser: seven leads, 40 deals, played to the end, with modeled players at every turn. The first run made modeled-player calls.

The percentages move a few points each run. They are estimates from 40 sampled deals against a model of the other players, not a solution of the game. What Walt acts on is the ranking, and the clear cases stay put: the 4-4 stays near the top and the 5-0 stays at the bottom.

The plain reason behind the extremes: the 5-0 is the lowest trump. Any other trump an opponent holds (the 5-3, 5-4 or 5-5) beats it, and that trick carries the 5-0's 5 points plus 1 for the trick. The 4-4 is the highest four. It loses only to a trump from someone with no fours left, so it usually wins the trick, keeps the lead, and saves Walt's trumps for later.

What the re-creation leaves out. It keeps the method and the tie rule but uses its own random number streams, so its deals differ from Walt's. Live Walt also runs a quick 8-deal pass first so it always has a move, uses 160 deals for the bidder's opening lead at the table, and may run a short partner check afterward. All of Walt is exploratory work. Its numbers are sampled estimates under a declared model of the other players, never solved values.
